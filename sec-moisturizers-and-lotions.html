    Section 2.4 Moisturizers and Lotions

    The previous section covers how body wash interacts with the stratum corneum and prevents stripped lipids. What happens when the skin is already damaged, chopped, or dehydrated? Water alone will not fix it — splashing water on dry hands and letting it evaporate simply leaves it feeling even drier than before. Instead, let’s explore how moisturizers help to repair the skin after dehydration.
    🔗

    Subsection 2.4.1 Plain Water Only Provides Momentary Relief

    Splashing water onto dry skin provides a momentary relief. However, water evaporates over time — as the ambient air evaporates that surface water, it pulls deeper moisture along with it.
    🔗
    There are two mechanisms that underlie this effect. First, since water is attracted to boundaries of containers, water molecules flow along tight spaces naturally without any external help — this is called capillary action. This is how paper towels absorb water. The second is diffusion — where substances naturally mix over time. If you drop food coloring in water, it will slowly diffuse into the water and mix.
    🔗
    These mechanisims accelerate the transepidermal water loss mentioned in Subsection 2.3.1.
    🔗
    In addition to drawing water out via evaporation, rinsing with water also leaches out hydrophilic compounds in skin cells, many of which are natural moisturizing factors.
    🔗
    This means that a moisturizer must not only deliver water to the skin. It must also trap the water in the cell matrix and replace the stripped molecules in the extracellular matrix.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.4.2 Humectants Pull Water from Deeper Layers of the Skin

    Modern moisturizing ingredients fall into a few large categories based on their functional role. The first is the humectants. Looking at Figure 2.4.1, we see that these molecules (with the exception of urea) contain many \(\ce{-OH}\) groups. These are called hydroxyl groups, and they are very hydrophilic due to the partial positive charge \(\delta^+\) on the hydrogen atom (similar to water, see Subsection 1.1.1 ).
    🔗
    The abundance of hydroxyl groups means that humectant molecules participate in a lot of hydrogen bonding. In urea, the nitrogen atoms play a similar role to the oxygen atoms in the other humectants.
    🔗
    When placed on the skin, humectants pull water out of the deeper layers of the skin and bind it directly to the dehydrated cells on the stratum corneum.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.4.3 Occlusives and Emollients Trap Water and Condition the Skin

    While humectants aid in absorbing water into the stratum corneum, water will eventually evaporate away, as mentioned in Subsection 2.4.1. It is equally important to seal this moisture in, and that is the role of occlusives.
    🔗
    As opposed to humectants, occlusives are long and hydrophobic. Petrolatum is also known as petroleum jelly, and is a mixture of long hydrocarbon chains. The US brand Vaseline contains many products involving petrolatum, and thus “vaseline” is often used synonymously with it.
    🔗
    When applied on skin, these sit on the very top of the skin to block transepidermal water loss.
    🔗
    Recall that when the skin dries due to evaporation, many natural moisturizing factors (NMFs) are removed from the skin. Emollients act by slipping into microscopic fissures in the skin, restoring suppleness and softness.
    🔗
    Emollients are typically medium-size lipids such as ceramides, squalene, and cetyl alcohol.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.4.4 Moisturizers Use Emulsifiers With Varying Oil-to-Water Ratios

    Recall from Section 1.1 that hydrophilic molecules (like water) exert strong cohesive forces and push nonpolar oil droplets out, causing spontaneous separation. Delivering humectants (hydrophilic) and occlusives/emollients (hydrophobic) in a single bottle requires forcing water and oil to coexist in a single bottle. How is this possible?
    🔗
    When two liquids that normally do not mix (i.e., they are immiscible ) are forcibly mixed together, an emulsion forms. In an emulsion, small droplets of one liquid are suspended throughout the other.
    🔗
    There are generally two types of emulsions that are relevant here. In oil-in-water (O/W) emulsions, tiny oil droplets are suspended in a continuous water phase. As water is the majority substance, O/W emulsions feel like water — light, non-greasy, and rinses easily. In water-in-oil (W/O) emulsions, tiny water droplets are suspended in a continuous oil phase. These feel richer, thicker, and deeply water-resistant.
    🔗
    But wait, why would the droplets not simply collide and coalesce together, eventually forming two distinct layers again? This is indeed what happens without the help of an emulsifier. Emulsifiers are amphiphilic molecules — molecules that have both hydrophilic and hydrophobic parts. Soap is an emulsifier.
    🔗
    Emulsifiers reside on the boundary between the two substances, forming a protective barrier around each droplet.
    🔗
    The words “lotion”, “cream”, and “ointment” refer to different oil-to-water ratios. They are listed in Table 2.4.7.
    🔗

    Example 2.4.8.

    Shake oil and water in a small jar, and observe — they will separate into two layers within 30 seconds. Now add a drop of emulsifier (dish soap, egg yolk, or mustard), shake again, and observe. What happens? Why?
    🔗
    Solution.
    The mixture now stays opaque, milky, and uniform.
    🔗
    The amphiphilic molecules in the emulsifier coat the microscopic oil droplets, preventing them from recombining. The same principle applies in cosmetic lotion products!
