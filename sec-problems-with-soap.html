    Section 1.3 Problems with Soap

    Traditional soaps (i.e., anionic surfactants), as described in the previous section, come with a few inherent drawbacks. This is why we only use hand soap to wash our hands. Many of these problems are resolved with modern synthetic detergents and cleansers. One simple example of a problem with traditional soap is the following: try to lather (bubble) up with soap and tap water. You might find that instead of bubbling, you get a weak lather and a dull white film. This is known as soap scum. Why does it form?
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.1 Unlike Synthetic Detergents, Soap Forms Scum in Hard Water

    Tap water often contains dissolved minerals, specifically calcium ions ( \(\ce{Ca^{2+}}\) ) and magnesium ions ( \(\ce{Mg^{2+}}\) ). We call this hard water.
    🔗
    The \(2+\) means that they are highly attracted to negative charges — including the negative heads of traditional soap molecules. Hence, soap molecules bind strongly to the charged minerals. Instead of dissolving in water to form micelles, the soap molecules just precipitate out as a solid, sticky curd called soap scum (made of calcium carboxylate ).
    🔗

    Example 1.3.1.

    Fill two small jars (call them jars A and B) with tap water. In jar B, dissolve a spoonful of Epsom salt (magnesium sulfate) to create artificial hard water. Add a few drops of liquid hand soap to both jars, cap them, and shake vigorously. What happens? Why?
    🔗
    Solution.
    In jar A, clear, thick foam forms due to the formation of micelles. In jar B, the magnesium ions disrupt micelle formation, forming cloudy water with less foam.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    To resolve the problem of soap scum, chemists invented synthetic detergents by tweaking the hydrophilic head. Instead of the carboxylate, which tightly binds to ions in minerals, detergents often use sulfonate or sulfate heads.
    🔗
    These heads do not tightly bind to calcium or magnesium ions. Thus, unlike traditional soap, these detergents remain soluble and lather freely, even in mineral-rich hard water.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.2 Traditional Soaps Are Highly Alkaline

    Traditional soaps are alkaline (the opposite of acidic), meaning they have a high pH level. pH is a measure of acidity. In normal circumstances, pH generally ranges from 0 (very acidic) to 7 (neutral) to 14 (very basic). Soap sits at a pH of around 9 to 10. You can see the pH of some household objects in Table 1.3.2.
    🔗
    Our human skin maintains a slightly acidic surface barrier (pH about 4.5 to 5.5) known as the acid mantle. High-pH bar soaps strip oils aggressively and disrupt the acid mantle, potentially damaging skin health.
    🔗
    Fortunately, modern synthetic cleansers, known as syndets, can be formulated at a skin-neutral pH to clean gently without disrupting the health of the skin barrier.
