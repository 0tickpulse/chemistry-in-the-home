    Section 1.1 Water and Oil

    You just finished a bucket of buttered popcorn. You run your hands under warm tap water, rub them, and... nothing happens. Your hands are still slick, and the water just beads up and rolls off.
    🔗
    Now sprinkle some salt on your hand, and watch it get instantly dissolved by the same water. Why?
    🔗

    Subsection 1.1.1 Water is Polar

    To understand why tap water simply bounces off your oily hand, we have to zoom in on what water actually is. If you have seen the symbols \(\ce{H2O}\text{,}\) you know that a single water molecule is made of two hydrogen ( \(\ce{H}\) ) atoms and one oxygen ( \(\ce{O}\) ) atom. The dynamics between three atoms lead to a property called polarity, in which one side of the molecule is positively charged, and the other is negatively charged.
    🔗

    Aside: Why is water polar?

    The atoms in water are bonded through covalent bonds. In a covalent bond, negatively charged particles called electrons are shared between the atoms. Oxygen is a notorious electron hog, and tends to pull electrons towards itself. This causes the electrons in the covalent bonds to cluster towards the oxygen, leaving the oxygen atom with a slight negative charge (we label this \(\delta^{-}\) ). The stripped hydrogens are left with a slight positive charge (we label this \(\delta^{+}\) ).
    🔗
    Water molecules are shaped like the letter V, or a pair of Mickey Mouse ears. We call this a bent geometry, and in our context it means that the positive and negative charges don’t cancel each other out. One side of the molecule is distinctively negative, the other side distinctively positive. This is precisely what polarity is — the separation of charge in a molecule.
    🔗
    A fundamental property of charged objects is that opposite charges like to attract, and like charges repel. In this sense, a water molecule is like a tiny bar magnet, whose poles are attracted to opposite poles of other water molecules (bottom of Figure 1.1.1 ). As one of the atoms involved is hydrogen, we call the attraction between water molecules hydrogen bonding.
    🔗

    Aside
    Just to clarify, hydrogen bonds are fundamentally different from covalent bonds, despite them both having “bond” in their names. Covalent bonds involve sharing electrons, whereas hydrogen bonds are simply attractive forces. We say that a molecule consists of atoms (typically) covalently bonded together, and different molecules may attract each other via hydrogen bonds. These are called intermolecular forces. As hydrogen bonds are weaker than covalent bonds, they do not make molecules.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.1.2 Oils are Not Polar

    In contrast to water, fats and oils are typically just long chains of carbon and hydrogen atoms that share electrons evenly. Due to the lack of polar bonds, oil molecules have no positive or negative side.
    🔗
    This means that there is no strong force of attraction between oil molecules (this is called cohesion; see Figure 1.1.2 ).
    🔗

    Example 1.1.3.

    One easy way to show that water and oil differ a lot in their cohesion is as follows.
    🔗
    Grab a single drop of water and place it on wax paper. Do the same with a drop of cooking oil. You will notice that the water drop is much more tall and dome-shaped ( Figure 1.1.4 ). This is because the strong hydrogen bonds in the water make the waterdrop pull in on itself. This is called surface tension. In contrast, the oil drop spreads out flatly due to the absence of hydrogen bonds.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.1.3 Salts Take Polarity to the Extreme

    In a salt, two atoms bond to each other. One of the atoms hogs the electrons so fiercely that instead of sharing electrons, the atom essentially takes them from the other atom. For example, the table salt you use is formed by sodium ( \(\ce{Na}\) ) and chlorine ( \(\ce{Cl}\) ). The chlorine atom takes one electron from sodium.
    🔗
    In a polar covalent bond, like in water, the electron spends more time in one of the atoms. There are only partial charges in the atoms. But in \(\ce{NaCl}\text{,}\) there is a distinctive extra electron that spends all its time around \(\ce{Cl}\text{,}\) meaning it has a full negative charge. We can emphasize this by writing chlorine as \(\ce{[Cl]^-}\text{.}\) Likewise, with a missing electron, the sodium has a full positive charge, so we write it as \(\ce{[Na]^+}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Something with a full charge is called an ion. Due to the fact that \(\ce{NaCl}\) contains full charges, the bonds between the atoms are much stronger than the hydrogen bonds between water. We call these bonds ionic bonds.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.1.4 Polar Substances Only Dissolve Other Polar Substances

    The reason water separates itself from oils is that the polar water molecules cling onto themselves so tightly that the nonpolar oil molecules get physically squeezed out. Water does not "hate" oil, it just loves itself too much to make any room for oil. For this reason, oil is called hydrophobic (i.e., water fearing ).
    🔗
    On the other hand, the \(\ce{[Na]^+}\) and \(\ce{[Cl]^-}\) ions are highly attracted to the polar water molecules, causing the salt to easily dissolve in the water. The positive sodium ions get held by the partially negative oxygen end of the water molecule; the negative chlorine ions get held by the partially positive hydrogen end of the water molecule. For this reason, table salt is called hydrophilic (i.e., water loving ).
    🔗

    Example 1.1.7.

    Grab some water and vegetable oil again, and optionally some table salt.
    🔗
    If you place the salt in the water, it easily dissolves. On the other hand, if you pour the oil, the water and oil separate cleanly into different layers.
    🔗
    Now stir the water and oil vigorously. What happens? Why?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Initially, the stirring forces the water and oil to mix. But they immediately separate again.
    🔗
    This is because the hydrophobic oil gets pushed out by water molecules that try to stick together.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    This is why your popcorn oil does not wash off with just water. The oil is hydrophobic and stays on your hand, forming a small layer above the skin. When water molecules reach your hand, they simply slide right off the oil layer.
    🔗
    In order to clean your hand thoroughly, you will need a way to wash off hydrophobic residues like oil. One such way is to use soaps.
    🔗
