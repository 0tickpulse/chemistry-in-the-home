    Section 4.1 Where do Colors Come From?

    Bleaching is a common process that involves the whitening of fabrics and the removal of stains. But to understand the chemistry involved in this mechanism, as well as how dyes work, we need to explore something more fundamental:
    🔗
    Where do colors actually come from?
    🔗

    Subsection 4.1.1 Light is a Wave

    For most of our history, we had no idea what light was. This was quite a troubling mystery — we knew nothing about light, yet our eyes used it all the time to examine the world. After centuries of work by physicists, chemists, and biologists, we now know that light is a wave (more specifically a traveling wave), and we have an understanding of how chemicals and our eyes interact with it. Let’s unpack what that means.
    🔗
    When we look at a single bit of light (called a photon ), it does three things:
    1. It is emitted by a light source like a light bulb;
      🔗
      🔗
    2. It travels across space; and
      🔗
      🔗
    3. It is absorbed by a light receiver like your eyes.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    The reason we call it a “wave” is that it has a few properties and phenomena associated with waves. Specifically, it has a wavelength, denoted with the Greek letter lambda \(\lambda\text{,}\) which tells us how long the distance between different peaks of the wave is. The wavelength is what determines the color of a photon.
    🔗
    Wavelengths span a spectrum called the electromagnetic spectrum, and our eyes can only perceive a small part of this spectrum. We call this the visible spectrum; see Figure 4.1.2.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.1.2 The Absorption of Light is Related to Electronic Transitions Between Molecular Orbitals

    As mentioned in the previous section, light must be emitted by a source and then absorbed to be observed. How does this actually happen?
    🔗
    Recall from Subsection 3.1.4 that radiation is one way thermal energy can be transferred. This is because photons themselves carry energy that is based on their wavelengths. The shorter the photon’s wavelength, the higher their energy is.
    🔗
    When electrons orbit an atom, they occupy spaces called atomic orbitals. When atoms combine to form a molecule, however, the atomic orbitals combine to form spaces called molecular orbitals, often abbreviated as MOs.
    🔗
    Each MO has a specific energy level, and electrons occupy them starting with the lowest energy level. They will occupy the MOs until there are no electrons left; the last electron occupies the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO). The orbital right above it is, as one would expect, the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO). You can see an example of a diagram with the HOMO and LUMO labeled in Figure 4.1.4.
    🔗
    Now suppose a photon is traveling towards the molecule. It carries a specific energy — let’s call it \(E\text{.}\) The molecule has electrons that occupy the HOMO. If \(E\) is exactly enough to boost the electron onto the LUMO, then the electron will absorb the photon. We say that it gets excited by the photon ( Figure 4.1.5 ).
    🔗
    Importantly, the energy of the photon must exactly be the energy of the energy gap between the HOMO and LUMO, no more and no less. If the energy does not match, it simply passes through the molecule or gets reflected.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 4.1.3 When One Color is Absorbed, the Complementary Color is Observed

    It may seem confusing why white light is not part of the spectrum in Figure 4.1.2. The answer is that white light is actually the combination of all wavelengths of visible light.
    🔗
    Now, suppose white light is shined onto a substance, parts of the white light might be absorbed. Let’s stipulate, for instance, that the substance absorbs yellow light. The rest gets reflected and observed by your eye.
    🔗
    Your eye receives a combination of every color other than yellow. This amounts to the color directly opposite to it in a color wheel, which is blue in this case ( see Figure 4.1.6 ). This opposite color is called the complementary color.
    🔗

    Example 4.1.7. Leaves and Chlorophyll.

    Leaves are green because there is a pigment called chlorophyll in it. What is the approximate wavelength that chlorophyll absorbs? Reference Figure 4.1.2 and Figure 4.1.6.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Looking at Figure 4.1.6, chlorophyll absorbs violet light, which has a wavelength of about 400 nm. Interestingly, this is not entirely accurate — chlorophyll actually absorbs both red and blue light, which mix to violet.
