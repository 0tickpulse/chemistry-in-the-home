    Section 1.5 Key Concepts

    1. Water is polar, meaning it has a partially positive side and a partially negative side.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Salts like sodium chloride ( \(\ce{NaCl}\) ) are ionic, meaning the atoms form full positive and negative charges.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Oils are nonpolar, meaning they do not have partially positive or negative parts.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Polar substances, like water, only dissolve other polar substances. Oil does not dissolve in water, so it is called hydrophobic. Salt dissolves in water, so it is called hydrophilic.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. Soap molecules are amphiphilic, meaning they have both hydrophilic and hydrophobic/lipophilic parts.
      🔗
      🔗
    6. When soap is dissolved in water, the molecules form spherical clusters called micelles that trap hydrophobic residues.
      🔗
      🔗
    7. Soaps act as surfactants by disturbing the surface tension of water, allowing it to reach tiny crevices in the skin.
      🔗
      🔗
    8. Soaps kill viruses with lipid bilayers by adsorbing onto the bilayer and destroying it completely.
      🔗
      🔗
    9. When exposed to hard water, traditional soaps form soap scum by binding to charged minerals. Modern synthetic detergents use different hydrophilic heads to resolve this.
      🔗
      🔗
    10. pH is a measure of acidity, with lower pH being more acidic.
      🔗
      🔗
    11. Traditional bar soaps are highly alkaline and may interfere with the acid mantle of the skin. Modern syndets can be formulated at skin-neutral pH to resolve this.
      🔗
      🔗
    12. Temperature is not a significant factor in handwashing.
      🔗
      🔗
    13. Antibacterial hand soaps do not offer significantly better bacteria removal than traditional soaps, and come with risks like contributing to antibiotic resistance.
      🔗
      🔗
    14. Hot-air dryers do not work as well as paper towels due to lack of friction, toilet particles in the air, and damp skin.
      🔗
      🔗
