    Section 2.1 The Structure of Hair

    Subsection 2.1.1 Hair Primarily Consists of Keratin Fibers

    Both the skin and hair, as well as nails, are part of a system in the body called the integumentary system. This system consists of all the things that cover and protect your body.
    🔗
    Hair grows from a hair follicle inside of the skin, but we will only be concerned with the exposed part of hair — the hair shaft. Starting from the outermost layer, a hair shaft consists of three distinct layers:
    1. The cuticle contains a few layers of dead cells that protect the inner layers. They are shaped like an armored pinecone. When the scales lie flat, hair reflects light (shine) and glides smoothly. Otherwise, the hair tangles, feels rough, and looks dull.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The cortex, which makes up most of the hair’s mass, is composed of something called keratin.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. The medulla makes up the center of the strand.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Much of the physical properties of hair have to do with what keratin actually is — a protein. In chemistry, we define a protein as a chain of small residues called amino acids. The amino acids are connected via covalent bonds (which are discussed in Subsection 1.1.1 ) called peptide bonds.
    🔗
    There are many different types of amino acids, each having a full name, a three-letter abbreviation, or a single-letter ID. They have vastly different properties, such as their hydrophilicity. The identity of these amino acids determines how the protein is structured in three dimensions.
    🔗
    Keratin, specifically, is structured as a superhelix — imagine the chain of amino acids forming a helix, and then that helix forming a bigger helix along its axis, bundled like a rope. The superhelix structure enables keratin to be extremely strong. A two-inch thick rope of human hair can support a small car.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.2 Hydrogen Bonds Account for the Temporary Structure of Hair

    Recall that hydrogen bonds are attractive forces between hydrogen and another atom that likes to hog electrons. Every amino acid contains an exposed hydrogen atom — therefore, hydrogen bonding can occur within the protein.
    🔗
    This hydrogen bonding accounts for much of the hair’s temporary structure. As soon as water touches the hair, however, the water molecules easily slip through the protein chain and disrupt the hydrogen bonds. When your hair then dries, new hydrogen bonds will form in whatever shape your hair was resting in.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.1.5. Morning Bedhead.

    One common situation where this comes into play is morning bedhead. While you sleep, the weight of your head against the pillow squashes your hair strands into weird, bent angles. If your hair is wet — either because you did not dry it thoroughly, or due to sweat or slight ambient humidity — then the hydrogen bonds are temporarily broken.
    🔗
    Then, as your hair sits, pressed against the mattress overnight, the water will eventually evaporate. New hydrogen bonds form and freeze the proteins in the bent, distorted shape.
    🔗
    You cannot brush bedhead out easily because these hydrogen bonds are locked in place. On the other hand, splash some water on your head, and the strands easily relax back to baseline.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.3 Disulfide Bridges Account for Permanent Hair Curl

    There is one amino acid, called cysteine (Cys or C), with a property that no other amino acid possesses. When two cysteine residues are within close proximity to one another, the sulfur atoms in each residue may actually bond to each other, forming a structure known as a disulfide bridge.
    🔗
    These occur periodically within hair, and manifest differently in different people. As disulfide bridges are covalent bonds, they are extremely strong, immune to water and moderate heat. Much of the unique natural hairstyle of a person is based on the presence of disulfide bridges.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.4 Hair is Also Held by Salt Bridges Which Are Sensitive to pH

    Some amino acids have side chains carrying positive electric charges, while others carry negative electric charges. When these opposite charges line up, they snap together through electrostatic attraction to form salt bridges (ionic bonds).
    🔗
    Unlike disulfide bridges, salt bridges are sensitive to changes in pH. At hair’s natural, slightly acidic pH (around 4.5 to 5.5), these ionic bonds stay firmly intact. However, if hair is washed with a high-pH (alkaline) substance like traditional bar soap, the positive charges are neutralized, breaking the salt bridges and causing hair strands to swell and frizz.
    🔗
    🔗
