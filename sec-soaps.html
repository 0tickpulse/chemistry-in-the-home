    Section 1.2 Soaps

    In the previous section, we established that hydrophobic residues, like oils, stay on your hand when treated with water. But the moment you introduce soap, they wash off immediately. Why?
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.1 Soaps are Amphiphilic Molecules

    Soap molecules have a distinctive property that make them excellent for cleaning purposes. A soap molecule is a long, linear chain of atoms. One end of it is very polar and hydrophilic, while the other end of it is hydrophobic.
    🔗
    The hydrophilic side (the head) typically contains a group of atoms called a carboxylate, which is negatively charged. The rest of the molecule (the tail) is a long chain of atoms called a hydrocarbon chain, similar to those in oil molecules.
    🔗
    Naturally, the soap molecule will arrange itself such that one side faces water molecules, and the other faces oils (this side is also called lipophilic for oil-loving ).
    🔗
    The fact that the soap molecule has hydrophilic and hydrophobic/lipophilic ends has a name — soap molecules are amphiphilic (for both-loving ).
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.2 Soaps Form Spherical Clusters Called Micelles When Dissolved in Water

    When you add soap to water, the hydrophilic heads are highly attracted to water, and the hydrophobic tails get pushed away from water molecules.
    🔗
    To minimize contact with water, soap molecules spontaneously assemble into tiny spherical clusters known as micelles. In a micelle, the hydrophobic tails point inwards towards the center, while the hydrophilic heads point outwards towards the surrounding water molecules ( Figure 1.2.2 ).
    🔗
    When you scrub your hand, you break the popcorn oil layer on your skin into tiny droplets, which then get trapped in these micelle cages.
    🔗
    As the outer layer of the micelle is hydrophilic, the micelle (containing hydrophobic residues) gets easily washed away into the drain.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.3 Soaps are Surfactants That Lower Surface Tension

    The story does not end with micelles. Due to the immense hydrogen bonds in the water, it has a significant amount of surface tension (recall Checkpoint 1.1.3 ). This means that it is difficult for water to reach and penetrate tiny crevices in your skin.
    🔗
    Soaps help by crowding around the water’s surface, interrupting the tight hydrogen bonds between water molecules. This significantly lowers the surface tension. For this reason, soap is also called a surfactant, short for surface-active agents — something that lowers the surface tension of the substance in which it is dissolved.
    🔗
    Specifically, since the carboxylate head is negatively charged, soaps are anionic surfactants (anionic means negatively charged).
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.2.3. Make Pepper Move.

    Sprinkle some pepper evenly onto a bowl of water. Dip a Q-tip or a toothpick in soap, and place it into the center of the bowl. What happens? Why?
    🔗
    Solution.
    The pepper instantly shoots outward toward the edges of the bowl.
    🔗
    This happens because the soap breaks the high surface tension at the center of the water bowl. The remaining water around the edges still has high surface tension and pulls the water (and floating pepper) away from the soap drop.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.4 Soaps Remove Microbes Through Varying Mechanisms

    In addition to dirt and other residues on your skin, there are microbes such as bacteria, viruses, and some fungi. Their cellular structures vary significantly, and soaps are able to kill or remove them to varying efficacy. There are two main categories of mechanisms by which soaps do so: (1) by trapping microbes in micelles, and (2) by disrupting the chemical structure of microbes.
    🔗
    Many microbial cells are encased by a structure similar to a soap micelle, known as a lipid bilayer. Viruses with lipid bilayers include coronaviruses (like Sars-CoV-2), influenza, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), and herpes.
    🔗
    If you only wash your hand with water, these microbes remain trapped in skin oils and survive completely intact. On the other hand, the moment you introduce soap, the molecules insert themselves (this is called adsorption ) into the microbes’ bilayers and completely destroy them.
    🔗
    For non-enveloped viruses without a lipid bilayer (like the norovirus or the rhinovirus, which causes the common cold), soaps cannot kill them directly. However, they still help remove the viruses by stripping away the oils that they cling to, allowing them to be flushed away with water.
    🔗
    Many other microbes, as well as bacterial spores and cysts, do not get directly chemically inactivated. Nevertheless, the physical lifting power of soap help remove them.
    🔗
